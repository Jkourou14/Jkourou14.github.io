*On restless minds, artificial intelligence, and the strange beauty of connecting things that were never supposed to belong together.*

There is something I have always found fascinating about the way the mind works. How one little thing can lead to another, and another, until a single thought becomes an entire universe of ideas. You can be reading about biology, stumble upon a concept in mathematics, remember a conversation you had three days ago, and somehow end up questioning the nature of intelligence itself. And the strangest part? Somewhere in your mind, all these things seem to belong together.

For some people, thinking feels like walking down a straight road. One thought follows another, each leading naturally to the next. But for others, the mind feels more like a city at night, with thousands of little lights turning on at once, each connected to another street, another memory, another possibility. You start with one thing and suddenly find yourself somewhere you never intended to go.

I have been thinking about this lately, especially when it comes to ADHD and large language models, or LLMs. At first, the comparison sounds a little absurd. One is a human brain, shaped by biology, emotions, hormones, memories, experiences, and the wonderfully inconvenient business of being alive. The other is a computational system trained on enormous amounts of text, learning mathematical relationships between words and concepts.

And yet, there is an interesting resemblance between the two. Both can produce an astonishing number of associations, recognize patterns across information, and connect ideas in ways that are not always immediately obvious.

Of course, they do not do this in the same way. But perhaps looking at their similarities can teach us something about intelligence, creativity, and the way we make sense of the world.

> “Sometimes, one little thought is all it takes to open a thousand doors.”

## The world is made of patterns, and our brains are always looking

We tend to imagine thinking as something deliberate. We sit down, concentrate, consider a problem, and eventually arrive at an answer. But a great deal of what happens inside our brains takes place before we are even aware of it.

Our brains are constantly detecting relationships between things. We learn to recognize familiar faces, anticipate the end of a sentence, notice changes in someone's voice, and connect present experiences to memories from years ago. We recognize patterns in music, language, movement, emotions, and even in the behavior of the people we love.

When you hear thunder and expect rain, your brain is drawing on a learned relationship. When you notice that a friend sounds different despite insisting that everything is fine, you are picking up on subtle cues. When a scientific observation reminds you of an entirely different mechanism you studied months ago, your mind is connecting information across contexts.

We rarely stop to appreciate just how much of our everyday intelligence depends on this ability.

Pattern recognition helps us navigate an overwhelming world. Rather than treating every experience as something completely new, our brains use what we have learned to interpret what is happening and anticipate what might happen next.

And this is where things become particularly interesting.

Large language models also learn patterns. During training, they process enormous collections of text and learn statistical relationships between words, concepts, and their surrounding contexts. When you give an LLM a prompt, it uses those learned relationships to generate a response, predicting what comes next based on the information available to it.

That does not mean it understands language exactly as a human does. Nor does it mean that its internal processes are equivalent to those of a biological brain. The underlying mechanisms are fundamentally different.

Still, both systems reveal something important about the role of patterns in processing information.

One is built from neurons, synapses, and a lifetime of experience. The other is built from mathematical operations and learned representations. Yet both can produce connections between pieces of information that, at first glance, seem surprisingly far apart.

And honestly, I think that is a fascinating place to begin.

## The ADHD mind: a thousand connections before breakfast

Anyone who has experienced the particular restlessness of an ADHD mind may recognize the feeling of having several conversations happening inside their head at once.

You are trying to finish one task, but a thought appears. That thought reminds you of something you read last week. The article reminds you of a scientific question. The question reminds you of a conversation. Suddenly, you have five new ideas, three things you need to look up, and absolutely no idea where your original task went.

It can be exhausting, but it can also be fascinating.

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition associated with differences in attention regulation, impulse control, and executive functioning. Its manifestations vary considerably from one person to another. Some people experience a constant stream of thoughts, while others struggle more with distractibility, task initiation, working memory, or regulating their attention.

Rapid associative thinking is not universal to ADHD, and it is not a diagnostic feature on its own. Nevertheless, many people with ADHD describe experiencing ideas as unusually interconnected, with one thought quickly activating several others.

A word can bring back a memory. A memory can inspire a metaphor. A metaphor can lead to a scientific hypothesis. And a scientific hypothesis can send you down an entirely unexpected rabbit hole.

Sometimes, this is experienced as distraction. Sometimes, it becomes curiosity, humor, creative writing, or the beginning of an interesting research question.

The same mind that struggles to stay with a routine task may become completely absorbed in a subject that captures its attention. The same person who loses track of what they were saying may notice an inconsistency that everyone else overlooked.

This does not mean that ADHD automatically makes someone more creative or intelligent. Research on ADHD and creativity is nuanced, and individual experiences differ. But it does invite us to think more carefully about the relationship between attention, association, and originality.

The question is not simply whether a mind can make connections, but also which connections it notices, how it evaluates them, and whether it can turn them into something meaningful.

> “A restless mind is not necessarily an empty mind. Sometimes, it is a mind trying to make sense of too many things at once.”

There is an important distinction here, though. Having many ideas is not the same as being able to organize them, evaluate them, or bring them to completion. That is often where the difficulties of ADHD become particularly frustrating. The mind may generate possibilities faster than the person can comfortably process them.

And that brings us to artificial intelligence.

## What happens when a machine learns from a universe of words?

Imagine asking an LLM about an apple.

Depending on the context, the response might involve nutrition, agriculture, mythology, the technology company Apple, the symbolism of fruit in literature, or the familiar story of Isaac Newton and gravity.

The word itself has not changed. What changes is the context in which it appears and the relationships the model has learned.

An LLM does not simply store a dictionary of isolated definitions. Modern models learn distributed representations that capture statistical relationships between words and concepts. Their attention mechanisms help them process relationships among elements of the input, while their learned parameters shape the patterns they use to generate language.

The result is a system capable of producing connections across subjects, styles, and domains.

Ask about cancer biology, and it may connect molecular pathways with clinical research. Ask about a scientific paper, and it may identify similarities between its methods and those used in another field. Ask it to explain a difficult concept through an everyday analogy, and it may draw on a completely different area of knowledge.

Sometimes the result is genuinely useful. Sometimes it is surprising. And sometimes, of course, the model makes a connection that sounds convincing but is incorrect.

That last part matters.

LLMs can generate plausible-sounding errors because their ability to produce coherent language does not guarantee that every claim is factually accurate. Their associations must be checked, especially when the subject involves scientific evidence, medicine, or other high-stakes decisions.

Still, their ability to combine learned information in flexible ways is remarkable.

A single prompt can activate patterns related to language, scientific concepts, narrative structures, and familiar examples. The system uses these relationships to construct a response that fits the context.

In that sense, an LLM can resemble an associative thinker;  one concept leads to another, and those relationships help shape what comes next.

But resemblance is not equivalence. A computational model does not have to experience curiosity, feel overwhelmed, or attach emotional significance to a memory in order to generate associations.

Its apparent train of thought should not be confused with the subjective experience of thinking.

## When an ADHD mind meets an artificial one

What interests me most is not the idea that ADHD brains and LLMs are the same. They are not. It is the possibility of examining two very different systems through a shared question: how do relationships between pieces of information help produce something new?

Consider what happens when a person with ADHD encounters an interesting scientific problem. They might begin by reading about a biological mechanism, then connect it to a mathematical model, then remember an observation from an unrelated paper. Suddenly, they see a possible relationship that deserves investigation.

An LLM can also help explore connections across these subjects. It might suggest related concepts, summarize unfamiliar terminology, propose alternative explanations, or help the researcher formulate questions worth investigating.

Both can contribute to a process of exploring possibilities. But they do so through different mechanisms, and neither guarantees that a promising association is a correct one.

The human researcher brings biological cognition, personal experience, scientific judgment, motivations, and responsibility for the work. The LLM contributes computationally learned patterns and generated suggestions. The human can test those suggestions against experimental evidence, reject them, refine them, and decide whether they are worth pursuing.

This is where I think the comparison becomes genuinely useful.

An LLM can serve as a kind of external thinking partner, particularly when someone wants to explore several interpretations of a concept. For a person who finds it difficult to capture a rapidly moving stream of ideas, it can also help organize scattered notes into a coherent structure.

But the goal should not be to replace the human mind with a machine. It should be to create a space in which ideas can be explored without losing sight of evidence, limitations, and the person doing the thinking.

There is something rather beautiful about that possibility: different forms of information processing working together, each with its own strengths and limitations.

## The beautiful side of seeing connections everywhere

Pattern recognition is not just a technical ability. It is woven into some of the things that make human life meaningful.

Science depends on recognizing relationships between observations and constructing explanations that can be tested. Mathematics explores structures and relationships that appear across seemingly unrelated problems. Music transforms patterns of sound into something that can move us. Storytelling connects individual experiences through themes, symbols, and shared emotions.

Even the way we understand other people depends partly on our ability to recognize patterns in their behavior, while remaining open to the possibility that we have misunderstood them.

A scientist notices that two biological processes may share a mechanism. A writer sees a metaphor hidden inside an ordinary moment. A child recognizes a familiar face in a crowd. An artist discovers that two colors evoke a feeling neither seems to produce alone.

In each case, the relationship between things becomes meaningful.

For someone who naturally thinks through associations, this can feel like living in a world where everything is connected to everything else. One subject is never entirely separate from another. There is always another question, another possible interpretation, another direction worth exploring.

But there is a delicate balance between noticing patterns and finding patterns where none actually exist.

Our brains are excellent at detecting relationships, sometimes even when those relationships are coincidental. This tendency, known broadly as apophenia, can lead us to see meaningful connections in random events. It is one reason scientific reasoning requires more than an interesting observation: it requires evidence, careful methods, and a willingness to be wrong.

The ability to generate a connection is only the beginning. The ability to examine it critically is what makes that connection useful.

And perhaps this is the part we should remember most. Creativity does not mean believing every idea that crosses our minds. It means allowing ourselves to explore possibilities while developing the discipline to distinguish a promising insight from an unsupported assumption.

## But a human mind is not a machine

For all their fascinating similarities, there is one difference I never want this comparison to obscure.

A human being does not simply process information. A human being lives.

Our thoughts are intertwined with our bodies, our emotions, our memories, our relationships, and everything we have experienced. A particular song can bring us back to a childhood afternoon. A familiar smell can make us miss someone we have not seen in years. A scientific idea can excite us because it reminds us of a dream we have carried for a long time.

These associations are not merely abstract relationships between concepts. They are part of a life unfolding over time.

ADHD can make this experience particularly complicated for some people. A mind that moves rapidly between ideas may also struggle with sensory overload, emotional regulation, sleep, or the demands of everyday responsibilities. An abundance of thoughts does not always feel like abundance. Sometimes, it feels like noise.

And when the mind refuses to slow down, even the most interesting idea can become one more thing competing for attention.

An LLM does not experience that same human burden. It can generate many associations without becoming mentally exhausted in the way a person can. It does not have a personal history that makes one memory painful and another comforting, nor does it have a human body whose needs must be balanced against the desire to keep thinking.

That distinction is essential. Comparing ADHD cognition with artificial intelligence can be intellectually interesting, but describing people with ADHD as computers, prediction engines, or machines with too many tabs open can never fully capture their experience.

We are not machines that happen to have emotions attached. Our emotions, bodies, and experiences are part of the very systems we are trying to understand.

> “The human mind does not merely connect ideas. It gives those connections a place in a life.”

## So, is intelligence really about connecting things?

I keep coming back to a question that seems deceptively simple: what makes an idea intelligent?

Is it the amount of information someone remembers? The speed at which they can process it? Their ability to solve a problem, recognize a pattern, or explain something that once seemed impossible to understand?

There is no single answer that captures every dimension of intelligence. Human intelligence involves many abilities, and pattern recognition is only one of them. Likewise, the impressive performance of an LLM in certain tasks does not establish that it possesses human-like consciousness or general intelligence in every sense.

But the ability to recognize and work with relationships is undeniably important.

Knowledge gives us information. Pattern recognition helps us identify relationships within that information. Reasoning helps us examine those relationships, and evidence helps us determine whether our conclusions hold up.

Perhaps the most interesting discoveries happen when these abilities work together.

An unexpected association might inspire a hypothesis, but testing that hypothesis is what moves us closer to understanding. A new analogy might make a complicated concept easier to grasp, but it must preserve the essential meaning of the original idea. A model might suggest a connection between two scientific fields, but researchers still need to determine whether the connection is real.

The magic, if we want to call it that, is not in making connections indiscriminately. It is in learning which connections matter.

And I find that particularly comforting when thinking about minds that do not always follow a straight line.

Not every detour is a discovery, and not every distraction hides a brilliant idea. Sometimes, a detour is simply a detour. But a mind that wanders can also encounter questions it would never have reached by following the most direct route.

The challenge is to create enough structure for those discoveries to become something tangible.

## Perhaps the point was never to think in straight lines

I do not think we need to romanticize ADHD to appreciate the complexity of the people who live with it. The difficulties are real, and the strengths are not guaranteed. Nor do we need to pretend that artificial intelligence thinks like a human in order to be fascinated by what it can do.

What we can do is remain curious.

We can ask what the similarities between biological and artificial information processing reveal about learning, creativity, and the role of relationships in understanding. We can explore how computational tools might support people whose ideas arrive faster than they can organize them. And we can recognize that intelligence is not a single, neatly packaged quality that looks identical in every mind.

Some minds move methodically. Others move associatively. Some people think best through words, others through images, movement, numbers, or a mixture of all four. Computational systems introduce yet another way of processing information, one that is powerful in certain contexts and limited in others.

None of these observations means that all minds are equivalent. It simply means that there is more than one interesting question to ask about how information becomes knowledge.

And perhaps that is what I love most about this subject: the opportunity to look at something familiar from an unfamiliar angle.

A wandering thought. A biological mechanism. A mathematical relationship. A language model generating its next sentence.

Different things, built on different foundations, yet all inviting us to ask how patterns shape what we can recognize, predict, and understand.

Maybe intelligence is not just about collecting facts or arriving at answers as quickly as possible. Maybe part of it lies in the questions we discover along the way, the relationships we learn to examine, and the courage to follow an idea without assuming we already know where it will lead.

Because sometimes, the most interesting thing about a mind is not how neatly it follows a path. It is what it notices along the way.

And sometimes, one little thought really does open a thousand doors.
