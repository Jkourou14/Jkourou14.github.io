Behind every medicine that reaches a patient, thousands of molecules never make it that far. Despite advances in drug discovery and clinical trial design, only around 10–15% of candidates successfully reach approval.

The main reasons include lack of efficacy, unexpected toxicity, and inadequate drug-like properties. A molecule may show promising results in the laboratory but fail to demonstrate meaningful clinical benefits in humans. Others may cause adverse effects or fail to reach the diseased tissue at sufficient concentrations.

But what if we are overlooking something important?

Drug discovery is not just about finding a molecule that binds strongly to its target. It is about understanding whether the target truly contributes to the disease, whether the drug acts through its intended mechanism, and whether it can reach the right tissue at an effective concentration without harming healthy organs.

This is where computational biology and in silico approaches can make a difference. By combining therapeutic target identification and validation, molecular modeling, quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling, and machine learning, researchers can investigate molecular interactions, predict biological activity and potential toxicity, and optimize drug candidates for potency, selectivity, and drug-like properties.

However, optimizing these properties alone may not be enough. A promising drug candidate must also achieve adequate exposure in diseased tissues while minimizing exposure in healthy organs. This balance between tissue distribution, efficacy, and toxicity is essential for successful drug development.

Emerging frameworks, such as the Structure-Tissue Exposure/Selectivity-Activity Relationship (STAR) approach, emphasize the importance of integrating drug potency with tissue exposure and selectivity to improve candidate selection and dose optimization.

Although computational methods cannot eliminate failure, combining them with experimental validation and tissue-specific pharmacokinetic assessments can help researchers make more informed decisions and prioritize candidates with a more favorable efficacy-toxicity profile.

The ultimate goal is to identify the right targets, optimize the right candidates, and increase the chances of bringing safe and effective treatments to patients.
