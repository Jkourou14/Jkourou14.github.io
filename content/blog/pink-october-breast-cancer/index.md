> Science helps us understand what is happening in the body. Compassion helps us remember whose body we are talking about… And we need both.

## Pink October should be about more than awareness

I have nothing against pink ribbons. In fact, symbols can be powerful. They can make an invisible issue visible, encourage conversations, and remind people that they are not alone. But awareness becomes limited when it stops at recognition.

We can share pink graphics every October and still avoid discussing the things that genuinely matter. We can tell women to take care of themselves without making healthcare accessible to everyone. We can celebrate survivors while overlooking the women who are still navigating treatment, the families struggling to support them, and the people whose experiences do not fit neatly into an inspirational story.

> Awareness should lead somewhere.

It should lead to better access to medical care, reliable information, appropriate screening, early evaluation of concerning symptoms, and support for patients throughout and beyond treatment. It should encourage us to question the barriers that prevent women from seeking care, whether those barriers are financial, geographical, cultural, or emotional.

And it should remind us that not every woman has the same opportunities to protect her health.

Telling someone to get screened is important, but we should also ask whether she can afford the appointment, reach a healthcare facility, obtain the recommended tests, and receive the follow-up care she needs. Individual responsibility matters, but healthcare systems and public health policies matter, too. If we genuinely care about women, we need to care about the conditions in which they are expected to look after themselves.

## The conversation we should be having with the women we love

Breast cancer awareness is also about the conversations we have in our own homes.

It is about encouraging our mothers, sisters, friends, and partners to pay attention to changes in their bodies without making them feel ashamed or frightened. It is about knowing our family histories and understanding that personal risk can vary. It is about seeking medical advice when something unusual appears instead of waiting for it to disappear out of fear.

A new lump or thickening, changes in breast shape or size, skin dimpling, nipple changes, or unusual discharge can warrant medical evaluation. These changes do not automatically mean cancer, but they deserve attention. Breast cancer can also occur without a noticeable lump, so knowing what is normal for your body is helpful, while professional assessment and age-appropriate screening remain essential.

The right screening schedule depends on factors such as age, family history, personal risk, and local medical recommendations. If you notice a concerning change, seek medical advice rather than waiting for your next routine screening appointment.

But beyond the medical advice, there is something else we can offer each other: a space where asking questions does not feel embarrassing.

We should be able to talk about breasts without reducing them to objects of desire. We should be able to discuss medical examinations without shame. We should be able to talk about cancer without treating the word itself as something that must be whispered.

> Our bodies are not inappropriate subjects for conversation simply because the conversation makes us uncomfortable.

And if we can discuss women's bodies in so many other contexts, surely we can learn to discuss their health with the same openness.

## A final thought, from one woman to another

This October, instead of only asking women to be strong, we can ask ourselves how to make their lives a little easier.

Instead of only celebrating courage, we can learn how to offer support without expecting gratitude in return. Instead of sharing another pink post and moving on, we can check on someone who is going through treatment, help someone find reliable medical information, or encourage someone we love to make an appointment she has been postponing.

And we can rethink the way we speak about femininity itself.

A woman is not defined by her breasts, but that does not mean she should be expected to feel indifferent if her body changes. She is not defined by her appearance, but she deserves to feel comfortable, beautiful, and at home in her own skin. She is not defined by a diagnosis, but she deserves to have every part of her experience taken seriously.

Pink October is a reminder that the body deserves care long before it becomes the subject of concern. It is a reminder that science, access to healthcare, compassion, and honest conversations all have a place in protecting women's health.

> And perhaps the most important thing we can take from this month is that awareness should never be seasonal when the people behind it are living their realities every single day.

So, this October, wear pink if you wish, share the ribbon, start the conversation; but remember the woman behind the symbol, because she deserves more than a month of awareness. She deserves care, dignity, reliable information, and the freedom to be a woman in all her complexity, on every day of the year.

*With love, and a little more awareness than yesterday.*
