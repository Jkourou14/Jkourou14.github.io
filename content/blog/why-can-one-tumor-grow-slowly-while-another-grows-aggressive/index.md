We often imagine cancer as a simple equation “cancer cells divide, the tumor gets bigger”. Yet, tumor growth is much more complicated  than that.

Think about two tumors developing in the same organ. They can have completely different growth rates, even though they are both technically “the same type” of cancer. The reason behind this is that cancer cells are not all biologically identical.

First, there are genetic alterations. Mutations and other genomic changes can activate pathways that tell cancer cells to keep dividing, while disabling mechanisms that normally stop abnormal cells or trigger their death. Changes affecting pathways such as RAS/MAPK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MYC, TP53, or cell-cycle regulation can therefore influence how quickly a tumor expands.

But mutations are only part of the story. A tumor also needs resources. Rapidly dividing cells need oxygen, nutrients, and energy. Aggressive tumors can stimulate the formation of new blood vessels through angiogenesis, helping them obtain the resources needed to continue growing.

Then comes the tumor microenvironment. Cancer cells do not live alone. They interact with immune cells, fibroblasts, blood vessels, and the extracellular matrix around them. Some of these cells can release signals that promote proliferation, invasion, or survival. Others can suppress the immune response, allowing cancer cells to escape destruction.

And the most interesting thing is “not every cell inside the same tumor behaves the same way.”

A tumor can contain different subpopulations of cancer cells. Some divide rapidly; others may enter a more dormant state. Some may be highly invasive, while others remain localized. As these populations evolve, the tumor can become more aggressive or develop resistance to treatment.

So when we ask, “Why is this tumor growing faster?”, the answer may involve several things happening at once, such as its genomic alterations, the signaling pathways driving cell division, its ability to obtain blood supply and nutrients, its interaction with the immune system, its tumor microenvironment, and the diversity of cancer cells within the tumor..

This is also why cancer cannot always be understood by looking at a single gene or mutation. The real question should be “What biological system is allowing this tumor to survive, adapt and keep growing?”
