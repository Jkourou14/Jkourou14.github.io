Drug repurposing: do we already have the medicine?

Have you ever gone to the doctor, and they prescribed you a medication for your illness… but you suddenly remembered: “Wait… I used to take this medicine for something completely different?” What you just experienced might actually have a scientific explanation called “drug repurposing”.

Drug repurposing means finding a new use for a drug that already exists.

A medication doesn’t necessarily have only one possible use. Sometimes, while studying a drug, scientists discover that it affects a biological mechanism that is also involved in another disease. So a drug originally developed for one condition becomes a potential treatment for another.

For example, Minoxidil was originally developed as a treatment for high blood pressure, but researchers noticed an unexpected side effect: increased hair growth. Today, it is widely used to treat certain types of hair loss…There are many other examples, and some are much more complex, but the main idea remains the same “an old drug can have a new story”.

But how do scientists actually find these new uses?

This is where things get really interesting, especially for people working in bioinformatics and computational biology. First, we try to understand the disease. What genes are dysregulated? Which proteins are involved? Which biological pathways are disrupted? Using data such as genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and other omics approaches, we can build a picture of what is happening inside the disease.

Then, we identify potential therapeutic targets. These can be proteins, receptors, enzymes, signaling molecules, or other biological components that might play an important role in the disease. Once we have these targets, we can look at existing drugs and ask: “Which drugs interact with these targets?” We can explore drug-target databases, scientific literature, pharmacological information, and computational predictions to find those connections.

And this is where we can start connecting the dots “drug, target, pathway, disease”.

Of course, finding a connection does NOT mean we have found a new treatment… We still need to evaluate how strong the evidence is... Scientists can combine omics data, drug-target interactions, protein-protein interaction networks, literature evidence, pharmacological information, and even AI-based predictions to prioritize the most promising candidates… Next comes one of the most important steps “validation”. Computational analysis can tell us, “This drug looks promising,” but laboratory experiments, preclinical studies, and clinical trials have to tell us whether it actually works and is safe for the new indication.

This is one of the most beautiful aspects of bioinformatics;  we don't always have to discover something completely new; we just have to look at what we already know from a different angle.
