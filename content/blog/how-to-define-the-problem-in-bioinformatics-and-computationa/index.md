Following my last post, I talked about how defining the problem in this field can feel complex. The problem is rarely obvious, we often rely on tools before truly defining what we are solving, and sometimes the impact of what we do is not immediately visible, especially in such a fast-evolving field, shaped by AI and constant discovery.

Today, I want to talk about something just as important: **how do we actually define the problem?** It’s not easy. But it’s not impossible either.

There are a few habits that, over time, make this process more natural.

The first one is simple, yet often underestimated: **curiosity.**

Not just reading dozens of articles, but allowing yourself to be curious , almost like a child discovering the world for the first time. Ask that simple question in your mind, even if it feels obvious. Ask why, when, where, what, which, how. They matter more than we often think. Let questions come freely, without overthinking whether they sound “good enough.” Clarity often starts there.

The next step is learning to be specific. Because specificity creates clarity.

Instead of saying: “study X”, “analyze X”, “explore X”…Try to refine your language: “identify”, “determine”, “calculate”

Then, define the context: Which disease? Which condition? Which group?

This is how a broad idea slowly becomes a focused question.

From there, ask yourself: **What am I actually measuring?** What result am I expecting?

Adding a comparison or a relationship (for example, A vs B) helps bring even more precision and direction.

Now comes the computational perspective.

Before going further, it’s important to ask: “Is this question feasible?” Do I have the data Can this be tested? Do I have the tools to approach it?

Because working on something for weeks and realizing it cannot be solved the way you imagined can be discouraging.

Next, translate your question into steps, almost like a small workflow: What data do I need? Which databases should I explore? What type of analysis would answer this question?

Also, just as important, be aware of the limitations of what you are doing. Not everything can be perfect or complete, and that’s part of the process.

In brief,  a “**precise question = objective + context + measurement + comparison”**

And maybe the most important reminder:

Precision in science, especially in bioinformatics and computational biology, doesn’t come from adding more, but from removing what is unclear.
