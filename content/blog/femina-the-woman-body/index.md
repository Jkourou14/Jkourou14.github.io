“The good physician treats the disease; the great physician treats the patient who has the disease.”
**William Osler**

But what happens when the patient has rarely been studied?

Medicine has transformed humanity in ways that would have seemed impossible only a century ago. Vaccines have prevented countless deaths, antibiotics have changed the course of infectious diseases, and artificial intelligence is beginning to reshape how we understand, diagnose, and treat illness. We have mapped the human genome, developed sophisticated imaging techniques, and learned to investigate diseases at molecular levels that earlier generations could barely imagine.

And yet, while reading scientific papers over the past few years, I kept coming back to an uncomfortable question. Why do so many women's diseases remain misunderstood? Why do some women wait years before receiving a diagnosis? Why are certain symptoms dismissed as stress, while others immediately raise medical concern?

The more I explored these questions, the more I realized that this is not simply a story about diseases that affect women. It is also a story about how science has evolved, what it has chosen to study, and what happens when the knowledge we consider universal is built on incomplete evidence.

This is not an accusation against medicine or the people who dedicate their lives to it. It is an invitation to look at scientific progress more critically. Because if medicine is supposed to understand the human body, then every body deserves to be part of that understanding.

## The woman who was told she had anxiety

Imagine a woman arriving at an emergency department feeling exhausted and nauseated, with an ache spreading through her jaw and up the back of her neck. She knows something is wrong. Her body is telling her that something is not right, but the symptoms do not match the picture she has been taught to recognize as a heart attack.

Perhaps she is told that it is stress. Perhaps anxiety is considered before a cardiac cause. Hours pass before the real problem becomes clear. She is having a heart attack.

The story is hypothetical, but the problem it illustrates is real. Women experiencing cardiovascular events may present with symptoms that differ from the classic signs many people associate with a heart attack. Chest discomfort can still occur, but fatigue, nausea, shortness of breath, and pain involving the jaw, neck, back, or other areas may also be important warning signs.

The difficulty is that medical knowledge does not exist in a vacuum. It is shaped by the patients included in research, the symptoms documented in textbooks, and the patterns clinicians are trained to recognize.

For a long time, the classic image of a heart attack was strongly associated with crushing chest pain, pain radiating down the left arm, and sudden collapse. These symptoms are important, but they do not describe every patient. When a woman's symptoms do not fit that familiar picture, the danger is that the symptoms themselves may be considered unusual rather than the medical model being incomplete.

The female heart was never less worthy of investigation. The problem was that our understanding of cardiovascular disease did not always reflect the full diversity of the people experiencing it.

And that raises a question that extends far beyond cardiology. How many other conditions have we misunderstood because we were looking for the wrong signals?

## How men became medicine's default

To understand the problem, we need to look backward.

For much of the twentieth century, men were disproportionately represented in many areas of biomedical research. Male animals were commonly used in laboratory experiments, clinical studies often enrolled predominantly male participants, and physiological models derived from male data were sometimes assumed to apply to everyone.

There were reasons behind some of these decisions. Researchers worried that hormonal fluctuations associated with the menstrual cycle would introduce variability into experiments. Pregnancy raised additional safety and ethical concerns, particularly following historical drug safety tragedies. Excluding women from certain studies was sometimes presented as a way to simplify research or protect potential participants.

But there is an important distinction between protecting people and leaving them insufficiently studied.

When biological variation is treated as an inconvenience, researchers may end up removing precisely the information they need to understand how the body works. Hormonal fluctuations are not simply noise. They are part of human physiology. Sex-related differences in immune function, body composition, metabolism, and organ function can influence how diseases develop and how treatments behave.

The consequences of underrepresentation are not limited to women being absent from research papers. They can affect the evidence used to establish diagnostic criteria, evaluate medications, identify risk factors, and make clinical decisions.

In the United States, concerns about drug safety and the exclusion of women from early clinical trials contributed to policies that restricted the participation of women of childbearing potential in certain studies. The US National Institutes of Health Revitalization Act of 1993 subsequently strengthened requirements for including women and minorities in NIH-funded clinical research.

That was an important step, but changing research policy does not automatically correct decades of accumulated assumptions. Scientific knowledge takes time to build, and the effects of earlier research decisions can remain embedded in the way we teach, investigate, and practice medicine.

Women were not excluded because their bodies mattered less in every researcher's mind. Rather, scientific systems repeatedly underestimated how much sex-related differences could matter.

And sometimes, the most consequential scientific blind spots are not created by a deliberate decision to ignore a question. They emerge because nobody realizes how much there is left to ask.

## Four conditions that reveal the gaps

Heart disease is only one example. Across several medical specialties, conditions affecting women have exposed the limitations of treating the human body as though one physiological model could explain everyone.

Consider endometriosis. It is a condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus grows outside the uterus, potentially causing inflammation, pelvic pain, painful menstruation, and fertility difficulties. Despite affecting millions of people worldwide, it has historically been associated with long diagnostic delays.

One of the most troubling aspects of endometriosis is how easily severe menstrual pain can become normalized. Many women grow up hearing that periods are supposed to hurt, that some people simply have worse cramps, or that pain is something to tolerate quietly.

But there is a difference between common menstrual discomfort and pain severe enough to interfere with school, work, relationships, sleep, or ordinary daily activities. Severe or persistent pain deserves medical evaluation, not automatic dismissal.

When pain is normalized, patients may delay seeking help, and healthcare professionals may underestimate the significance of what they describe. The result can be years of uncertainty before a condition is recognized.

The word *normal* can be reassuring when it is accurate. When it is used without sufficient investigation, it can also become a barrier to diagnosis.

Then there is polycystic ovary syndrome, commonly known as PCOS. It is one of the most common hormonal conditions affecting women of reproductive age, yet its complexity can make it difficult to recognize consistently.

PCOS does not necessarily look the same in every patient. Its manifestations can involve menstrual irregularities, androgen-related symptoms, reproductive health, and metabolic function. Some women primarily seek help for irregular periods, while others are concerned about acne, unwanted hair growth, difficulty conceiving, or metabolic changes.

When these symptoms are considered separately, the connection between them can be missed. A patient may find herself moving between different consultations, addressing individual problems without receiving a clear explanation of the broader pattern.

This is one of the reasons understanding hormonal conditions requires more than looking at a single symptom or laboratory result. The human body operates through interconnected systems. Hormones do not function in isolation, and reproductive health cannot always be separated from metabolism, skin, mood, or long-term health.

PCOS reminds us that a condition can be common and still be misunderstood, especially when its manifestations vary from one person to another.

Autoimmune diseases raise another fascinating question. Why do so many of these conditions disproportionately affect women?

Diseases such as lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, and Sjögren's syndrome occur more frequently in women than in men. Approximately four out of five people living with autoimmune diseases are women, although the exact proportion varies by condition.

The reasons are complex. Researchers are investigating the contributions of sex hormones, genetics, immune regulation, environmental exposures, and other biological factors. No single explanation accounts for every autoimmune disease, and important questions remain unanswered.

The immune system is designed to recognize and defend the body against threats. In autoimmune disease, immune responses become directed against the body's own tissues. Understanding why this happens more often in women could help researchers uncover mechanisms that influence not only autoimmune conditions but also the broader relationship between sex and immune function.

These examples are different, but they share an important lesson. Sometimes, the problem is not that a disease does not exist in our scientific knowledge. It is that we do not yet understand its full complexity, recognize its manifestations consistently, or know why it affects certain populations differently.

And that is exactly why research must continue to evolve.

## Women are not simply smaller men

There is a persistent misconception that women can be treated as smaller versions of men when it comes to medical research and treatment.

Biologically, that assumption does not hold up.

Sex-related differences in body composition, hormones, organ function, enzyme activity, and immune responses can influence the way a medication behaves in the body. A drug must be absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and eliminated. Differences in these processes can influence drug exposure, effectiveness, and the risk of adverse effects.

This does not mean every medication requires a separate dose for women and men. It means that differences should be investigated rather than assumed to be irrelevant.

A treatment that works well in one population cannot automatically be presumed to have identical effects in every other population. The appropriate dose and safety profile depend on the specific drug, the individual patient, and the available evidence.

Historically, women have been underrepresented in many clinical trials, and pregnant women have frequently been excluded because of concerns about potential harm to the fetus. These precautions can be important, but exclusion also creates a difficult problem: clinicians may need to make treatment decisions without sufficiently strong evidence about the benefits and risks of particular medications during pregnancy.

This leaves us with a genuine scientific and ethical challenge. How can medicine protect patients while also generating the evidence needed to treat them safely?

The answer cannot be to ignore safety concerns. Nor can it be to leave entire populations permanently underrepresented in the evidence base. We need carefully designed studies, appropriate safeguards, and research strategies that address the needs of different populations.

Medicine should not assume that biological differences automatically mean different treatments. It should investigate those differences carefully enough to know when they matter.

That is the difference between making assumptions about a patient and practicing evidence-based medicine.

## The invisible bias: when pain is not believed

There is another dimension to this problem, and it cannot be explained by biology alone.

Sometimes, the gap lies in perception.

Pain is one of medicine's most subjective symptoms. A blood test can produce a numerical result, an imaging scan can reveal a structural abnormality, and a biopsy can provide evidence at the cellular level. Pain, however, must often be understood through a combination of the patient's account, clinical examination, investigations, and the broader medical context.

That makes the way we listen especially important.

Research has documented patterns in which women presenting with pain may wait longer for pain relief in some emergency settings or have their symptoms attributed to psychological causes. These patterns do not describe every clinician, every hospital, or every patient. But they are important because even subtle differences in how symptoms are interpreted can influence the course of care.

Imagine describing persistent pain and repeatedly hearing that it is probably stress. Imagine explaining that something has changed in your body, only to wonder whether you are exaggerating because the people around you do not seem concerned.

Over time, the experience can become more than a medical problem. It can affect how a person understands her own body and whether she trusts herself enough to seek help again.

The difficult part is that bias does not always look like hostility. It can exist in familiar expressions, assumptions learned during training, outdated teaching materials, and the expectations we carry into a consultation. It can influence the judgment of any clinician, including women physicians, because unconscious assumptions are not exclusive to one group.

Recognizing these patterns is not about accusing every doctor of discrimination. Healthcare professionals work under enormous pressure, often with limited time and incomplete information. Diagnosis is complicated, and not every unexplained symptom indicates that something serious has been missed.

But these realities make it even more important to understand how bias can interact with uncertainty. When a symptom is unfamiliar, the right response is not necessarily to assume the worst. It is to remain curious, investigate appropriately, and avoid prematurely closing the door on possible explanations.

A patient's report is not a substitute for clinical investigation, but neither should it be dismissed simply because it does not fit an expected pattern.

Listening is not separate from science. In medicine, it is often where the investigation begins.

## Can artificial intelligence succeed where medicine fell short?

As someone fascinated by bioinformatics and computational biology, I find this question particularly interesting.

Artificial intelligence is increasingly being explored as a tool for identifying patterns that may be difficult to detect through traditional approaches. By analyzing large datasets, computational models can help researchers investigate disease risk, identify potential biomarkers, explore biological mechanisms, and predict how patients may respond to certain treatments.

In women's health, these methods could help researchers search for biomarkers associated with endometriosis, investigate pregnancy complications, explore sex-specific patterns of disease progression, and improve the personalization of treatment.

Consider the possibilities.

A model trained on sufficiently diverse clinical and molecular data could help identify combinations of features that distinguish one disease from another. Instead of relying exclusively on a single symptom, researchers could investigate patterns involving clinical history, laboratory measurements, genetics, hormones, and other biological information.

The same principle applies to drug development. If researchers can better understand how patients differ in their responses to treatment, computational models may help identify groups that need different approaches or closer monitoring.

The broader promise is a shift in the questions we ask. Instead of focusing only on what works for the average patient, we could increasingly investigate what is most appropriate for a particular individual.

This is one of the ambitions of precision medicine.

But there is a catch, and it is a significant one.

An algorithm is only as informative as the data, assumptions, and validation behind it.

If women are underrepresented in the datasets used to develop an algorithm, the model may perform less accurately for them. If historical diagnostic patterns contain bias, a model may learn to reproduce those patterns. If certain symptoms have been systematically overlooked, the resulting data may fail to capture the full range of disease presentations.

In other words, artificial intelligence can reproduce the blind spots it was supposed to help us overcome.

A sophisticated model does not automatically produce an unbiased answer. Technical complexity cannot compensate for systematically missing information.

This is why developing better computational tools must go hand in hand with building representative datasets, evaluating model performance across different populations, and validating predictions in real clinical settings.

We should also be careful about what these technologies can currently achieve. AI can support research and clinical decision-making, but it does not automatically replace clinical expertise, eliminate uncertainty, or guarantee earlier diagnoses.

The future of women's health will not be transformed by algorithms alone. It will depend on the quality of the questions we ask, the patients represented in our data, and the care with which we interpret the answers.

For computational biologists, bioinformaticians, clinicians, and researchers, this creates both an opportunity and a responsibility. We are not simply building models to recognize patterns. We are helping determine which patterns become visible to medicine in the first place.

## What would medicine look like if every body mattered?

Imagine walking into a hospital twenty years from now.

Your medical history is available to the healthcare team. Your clinical data are interpreted alongside relevant genetic, hormonal, and metabolic information. Digital tools help clinicians identify patterns, recognize potential risks, and evaluate treatment options tailored to your circumstances.

Perhaps wearable devices contribute information about changes in your daily physiology. Perhaps advances in molecular medicine help distinguish between diseases that previously appeared almost identical. Perhaps a clinician can use validated computational tools to identify warning signs before a condition becomes more difficult to treat.

The most important part of this future would not be the technology itself.

It would be the question guiding its use.

Instead of asking only, "What works for most patients?", medicine would also ask, "What works for this patient, given her biology, history, circumstances, and needs?"

That future will require much more than artificial intelligence. It will require inclusive clinical research, better representation of women across different stages of life, stronger investigation into sex-related differences, and medical education that reflects the diversity of human physiology.

It will also require us to acknowledge that women are not a single, uniform research population. Age, genetics, hormonal status, pregnancy, menopause, environmental exposures, and many other factors influence health. Understanding women's bodies means investigating that diversity rather than replacing one oversimplified model with another.

There is no single female body that can serve as the universal reference for all women. There are individual bodies, each with its own biological history and clinical context.

And the same principle applies to humanity as a whole.

## A final thought: science must remain curious

I started thinking about this subject because I kept encountering questions that did not seem to have simple answers. Why are some diagnoses delayed for years? Why do certain diseases disproportionately affect women? Why can the same medication produce different outcomes in different patients? And why does a scientific field as advanced as medicine still struggle to understand conditions that affect millions of people?

The more I thought about these questions, the more I realized that scientific progress is not only about discovering new things. It is also about recognizing the limitations of what we already believe we know.

Every scientific model is built from evidence. Every textbook reflects the research available when it was written. Every diagnostic guideline represents an attempt to organize complex biological reality into something clinicians can use. These tools are essential, but none of them should become immune to revision.

Science moves forward when we question assumptions, investigate inconsistencies, and remain willing to change our minds when the evidence tells us to.

Women's health is an important reminder of that principle. It shows us what can happen when a population is insufficiently represented in research, when symptoms are interpreted through narrow expectations, and when biological complexity is treated as an obstacle rather than an opportunity for discovery.

Understanding women better does not only improve women's healthcare. It improves medicine itself.

Because the goal was never to understand one type of human body and assume the rest would follow.

The goal was to understand the human body.

And perhaps the next major advance in medicine will not come only from discovering something entirely new. Perhaps it will also come from looking again at the questions we thought we had already answered, listening more carefully to the people we treat, and recognizing the knowledge that was missing all along.

**There is no default human body. There are individual ones. And every one of them deserves to be understood.**

*With curiosity,*
*Jouhaina Kourou*
