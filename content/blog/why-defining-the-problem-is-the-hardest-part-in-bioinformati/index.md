As a bioinformatician or computational biologist, I often find myself in the middle of data and tools, feeling lost, almost trapped, until I pause and ask myself: “What on earth am I actually trying to solve?”

And I’ve come to realize that this is almost a canonical moment in this field. Because defining the problem is often harder than coding itself.

In bioinformatics and computational biology, the problem is rarely obvious. Biology, by nature, is complex and messy. The same question can be framed in multiple ways, each leading to different methods, different analyses, and different results…And that doesn’t mean you are doing it wrong.

One of the main reasons is that we often start with data and tools rather than with the question. We fall into tool-driven thinking instead of question-driven science. It’s a natural tendency, but it’s also where confusion begins.

There is also the interdisciplinary challenge. This field sits at the intersection of biology, mathematics, statistics, computer science, and medicine. Each discipline brings its own way of defining what matters. And somehow, we are expected to bring all of that together into one clear, precise question…That’s not easy.

Another layer of difficulty is that impact is not always immediately visible. You can spend time building something technically solid, yet biologically irrelevant. And as we think, refine, and build, the problem itself evolves.

Sometimes, by the time you finish addressing one question, new discoveries have already shifted what matters most.

Defining a computational problem also requires understanding what “correct” looks like, which means going beyond code and deeply understanding the biology behind it. It requires clarity in thinking, critical evaluation, and the ability to make sense of ambiguity.

Often, the real gaps are not obvious. They hide in contradictions between studies, biases in datasets, unchallenged assumptions, or questions people avoid because they are complex and uncomfortable.

And yet, many of us carry the mindset that “The question has to be complex to be valuable.”

But in reality, the most powerful questions are often the simplest, clear, precise, and sharply defined.

And maybe that’s something we need to remind ourselves more often, we shouldn’t be afraid to ask simple questions.
