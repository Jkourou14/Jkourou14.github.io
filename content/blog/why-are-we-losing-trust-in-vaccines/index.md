For many of us, diphtheria belongs to another time. It is a disease we associate with old medical textbooks, previous generations, and a period when infectious diseases were much more visible in everyday life. Yet recent events in Morocco are reminding us that vaccine-preventable diseases have not simply disappeared. On September 28, the Moroccan Ministry of Health reported two limited family clusters of diphtheria in the Souss-Massa region. One case was laboratory-confirmed, while eight additional cases were under investigation, including two deaths… According to the Ministry, the children concerned had either never been vaccinated against diphtheria or had not completed the recommended vaccination schedule. The authorities have since initiated epidemiological investigations, contact tracing, preventive measures, and vaccination activities.

Beyond the immediate response to these cases, however, there is a broader question that deserves our attention: “Why are some parents becoming increasingly hesitant about childhood vaccination?” It would be easy to dismiss vaccine hesitancy as ignorance or a rejection of science, but the reality is much more complicated. Most parents who worry about vaccines are not trying to harm their children. In many cases, their hesitation comes precisely from the desire to protect them. They encounter stories about adverse effects, alarming videos on social media, contradictory opinions, and claims that are difficult to distinguish from reliable information. For many families, the COVID-19 pandemic intensified these concerns. Vaccines suddenly became part of everyday political, social, and media debates, scientific recommendations evolved as new evidence became available, and misinformation spread at an extraordinary speed. For some people, this experience strengthened confidence in scientific research; for others, it created or deepened mistrust.

Another layer of this problem is the growing presence of conspiracy theories around vaccination. Some of these narratives suggest that vaccines are deliberately harmful, that pharmaceutical companies are hiding dangerous effects, that governments are using vaccination programmes for purposes unrelated to public health, or that scientists and healthcare professionals are part of a larger system designed to conceal the truth. These ideas can be particularly powerful because they offer simple explanations for scientifically and socially complex situations. They also tend to spread through stories that feel personal and emotionally convincing, often presenting themselves as information that “they” do not want people to know. Once distrust toward institutions is established, almost any new piece of information can be interpreted as evidence that the conspiracy exists, while information that contradicts it may itself be dismissed as part of the cover-up. This does not mean that institutions, pharmaceutical companies, or scientists should be beyond criticism; transparency and accountability are essential to science and public health. But there is an important difference between questioning institutions and accepting a claim simply because it challenges them. A healthy scientific culture should leave room for skepticism while still requiring evidence strong enough to support the conclusions we draw from it.

This is why simply telling people to “trust science” is not always enough. Trust is built through transparency, communication and evidence. Parents deserve to understand what a vaccine is, what benefits it provides, what side effects are known, how rare serious adverse reactions are, how safety is monitored and what happens when a potential safety signal is detected. Vaccines, like any medical intervention, are not completely free of adverse effects. The scientifically accurate position is not that vaccines have zero risks, but that their risks are continuously studied and monitored and must be evaluated in comparison with the risks associated with the diseases they prevent. The World Health Organization emphasizes that vaccine safety monitoring continues after vaccines are introduced, and that an event occurring after vaccination does not automatically mean that the vaccine caused it. Establishing causality requires systematic investigation and appropriate epidemiological evidence.

This distinction is particularly important in the age of social media, where personal stories can spread much faster than scientific evidence. If a person experiences a health problem after receiving a vaccine, the temporal relationship is real and deserves to be investigated, but it does not by itself establish causation. The same principle applies throughout medicine and research: two events occurring close together in time are not necessarily biologically connected. Scientific investigation exists precisely to distinguish coincidence, correlation and causation. Dismissing a reported adverse event is not good science, but neither is automatically attributing every event following vaccination to the vaccine itself.

At the same time, it is important to remember what vaccination has achieved. Diphtheria is a contagious bacterial infection that can cause serious complications, including respiratory obstruction, heart damage and neurological complications. It is also a vaccine-preventable disease. Morocco's vaccination programme has contributed to keeping diseases such as diphtheria under control for decades; according to the Moroccan Ministry of Health's public health information portal, no diphtheria cases had been recorded in the country since 1991 before the recent events. This success creates an interesting paradox in public health: when prevention works for a long time, the disease becomes almost invisible, while the intervention that prevents it remains visible. A parent may therefore see a vaccine being administered to a healthy child without ever having seen the disease that vaccine is preventing.

This does not mean that parents should stop asking questions. On the contrary, questions are an essential part of science. The challenge is making sure that those questions are answered with reliable evidence rather than anecdotes, fear-based content or misleading claims circulating online. Parents should be able to ask healthcare professionals about the benefits and risks of each vaccine, possible contraindications, the recommended schedule and what to do when a dose has been missed. They should also be able to access clear information from public-health institutions rather than having to navigate an overwhelming mixture of scientific information, personal testimonies and misinformation on social media. In Morocco, the Ministry of Health has been encouraging parents and guardians to check children's vaccination records and complete missing doses according to the national immunization programme.

There is therefore an important distinction between questioning vaccination and rejecting evidence. Asking whether a vaccine has risks is reasonable. Asking how those risks are monitored is reasonable. Asking how clinical trials work and how safety signals are investigated is reasonable. What becomes dangerous is making a medical decision based solely on an isolated story, a viral video or a claim whose source and evidence cannot be established. Scientific literacy means learning how to evaluate information, not simply deciding in advance that every institution is right or every institution is wrong.

The recent diphtheria cases should therefore not become an opportunity to shame parents who are afraid. Fear rarely disappears when people are ridiculed. Instead, this moment should encourage a broader conversation about how we communicate science and public health in Morocco. Scientists, physicians, journalists, educators and health institutions all have a role to play in making reliable information understandable and accessible. Parents, meanwhile, deserve the opportunity to make informed decisions based on evidence, with their concerns taken seriously rather than dismissed.

Vaccination is ultimately not a question of blindly trusting science. Science does not ask us to believe without evidence; it asks us to examine evidence, question it, test it, reproduce findings and revise our understanding when better evidence emerges. The goal should not be to eliminate questions, but to make sure that when parents have questions about their children's health, the most accessible answers come from good evidence rather than misinformation.

Vaccine success can make the diseases it prevents almost invisible, and when that happens, maintaining public confidence becomes as important as maintaining vaccine coverage. Protecting children requires more than providing vaccines; it also requires building an environment in which parents can ask difficult questions, healthcare professionals can answer them honestly, and scientific evidence can compete with misinformation on equal terms. Public health depends not only on prevention, but also on the trust and understanding that allow prevention to work.
