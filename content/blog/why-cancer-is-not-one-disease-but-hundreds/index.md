“Cancer” sounds like a single disease.

But biologically…It is closer to hundreds of different diseases hiding under the same name.  

When we hear “This person has lung cancer” or “She was diagnosed with breast cancer,”… It sounds straightforward. As if cancers are clearly defined categories with one cause and one solution. 

But the reality is far more complex. Two patients may both have “lung cancer” and still respond completely differently to the same treatment.

Why?

Because cancers are not only defined by where they appear in the body. They are also defined by the mutations driving them, the molecular pathways involved, the subtype, the stage, how aggressive they are, and even how they interact with the immune system.

In simple words, not all cancer cells behave the same way. Some grow slowly, others spread aggressively. Some depend on specific mutations, others evolve multiple escape mechanisms. Some respond to therapy, others become resistant almost immediately.

Even inside the same tumor, cancer cells are often genetically different from one another. Which means a tumor is not always one uniform mass…It can behave more like an evolving ecosystem of competing cell populations.

And this is one of the reasons cancer treatment is so difficult. Because doctors are not simply treating “a lung cancer” or “a breast cancer.”

They are trying to treat a very specific biological version of that cancer. For example, two patients may both have breast cancer, but one may have “HER2-positive breast cancer”, while another has  “triple-negative breast cancer”. These subtypes behave differently, spread differently, respond differently to drugs, and have completely different treatment strategies.

The same applies to melanoma, leukemia, colorectal cancer, and many others.

Even the stage matters enormously. A localized tumor is biologically and clinically very different from metastatic cancer that has already spread throughout the body.

And then comes another layer of complexity that “cancer changes over time”. As cancer cells divide, they accumulate new mutations; some cells die, whereas others survive treatment and adapt, which means the cancer a patient has today may not be biologically identical months later.

In many ways, cancer behaves less like a static disease…Instead, more like a constantly evolving system.

This is also why there will probably never be “One universal cure for cancer.” Because treating cancer is not solving one problem, but rather solving hundreds of biological problems at the same time.

And this is what makes oncology both fascinating, complex and humbling…The deeper science looks into cancer, the more complexity it discovers.

Not because science is failing…But simply because biology is far more complex than we once imagined.
